PROBABILITY · NEW INFORMATION

Given an event, which outcomes remain?

A conditional probability changes the question by telling us something has happened. First count within the smaller set of possible outcomes; then use the same idea to read Bayes’ rule.

Restrict the sample space first.

Roll one fair six-sided die. Each face from 1 through 6 is equally likely. Let B mean “the roll is even,” with outcomes 2, 4, 6. Let A mean “the roll is at least 4,” with outcomes 4, 5, 6. What is the chance of A given that B happened?

One fair roll: keep only the even faces after learning B
Face123456
In B (even)?NoYesNoYesNoYes
In A (at least 4)?NoNoNoYesYesYes

Within B, three equally likely faces remain: 2, 4, 6. Of these, 4 and 6 also belong to A. The intersection means outcomes in both events. Here A∩B={4,6}. The conditional probability is therefore two of the three remaining faces:

GIVEN BP(A|B)=23
READ IT ALOUD

The probability that the roll is at least four, given that it is even, is two out of the three even faces. The vertical bar means “given that,” not division.

Without the information B, three of all six faces satisfy A, giving P(A)=36=12. That answers a different question. Learning B removes faces 1, 3, and 5; it does not change what occurred on the die.

Why divide the overlap by the given event?

Out of all six equally likely faces, the overlap has 2 and B has 3. The two probabilities are P(A∩B)=26,P(B)=36. Divide them: the common six-outcome denominator cancels, leaving 2 of the 3 outcomes in B.

CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY
P(A|B)=(26)÷(36)=23
P(A|B)=P(A∩B)P(B)
READ IT ALOUD

The chance of A given B is the chance of both A and B, divided by the chance of B. We keep the outcomes in B and count which also satisfy A.

The second line also works for unequal outcome probabilities: it compares probability mass in the overlap with all probability mass in B. It requires P(B)>0. If B is impossible, there is no ordinary conditional probability to calculate; if B is certain, conditioning leaves the original chance unchanged. Nothing here assumes A and B are independent.

Reverse a given question: Bayes’ rule

Put 12 distinguishable tickets in a bag and draw one uniformly. Four tickets are red (R), eight are blue (not R). Three red tickets and two blue tickets have a star (S). A red ticket has a 3-out-of-4 chance of a star; if the drawn ticket has a star, what is the chance it is red? These are different directions of conditioning.

All 12 equally likely tickets, grouped by color and star
ColorStarNo starTotal
Red (R)314
Blue (not R)268
Total5712

After learning S, only 5 star tickets remain, 3 of them red. So P(R|S)=35. To express the same count using earlier probabilities, the red-and-star overlap has 3 of the 12 tickets. Multiply the fraction that are red by the fraction of red tickets with stars:

COUNT THE OVERLAPP(R∩S)=P(S|R)P(R)=34×412=312
READ IT ALOUD

Red and star is the chance of red, times the chance of star given red. Here that is four twelfths times three quarters: three twelfths of all tickets.

This multiplication follows from the conditional rule above, by multiplying both sides by P(R). It is not an independence assumption. The star tickets come from two disjoint groups (red and blue): 3 plus 2 makes 5, so P(S)=512. Divide the red-and-star chance by the chance of any star:

BAYES’ RULE
P(R|S)=(312)÷(512)=35
P(R|S)=P(S|R)P(R)P(S)
READ IT ALOUD

To find red given star, take star given red times the chance of red, then divide by the chance of any star. The five star tickets—not all twelve—are the possible outcomes after learning that the ticket has a star.

This is Bayes’ rule: the conditional-probability rule applied to the same overlap in the opposite order. The letters R and S stand for these two events; the same relationship works for other events when both event probabilities are positive. Here P(R)>0,P(S)>0, so both conditionals are defined. Do not swap the two conditionals: star given red is 34, while red given star is 35. If the group sizes or star counts change, the answer changes too.
