PRACTICE · PROBABILITY AND COUNTING

Cards: pairs and two pairs

How often does a five-card hand contain exactly one pair—or exactly two pairs? Build each favorable count from choices of ranks and suits, then compare it with every possible hand.

SUBJECTS USEDProbabilityCombinationsFactorials
THE MODEL

Rules and assumptions

  • Use a standard deck of 52 distinct cards: 13 ranks, four suits per rank, no jokers.
  • A hand contains five cards drawn without replacement. We count the hand, not the dealing order.
  • Every five-card hand is equally likely under a uniformly shuffled deck.
  • “Exactly one pair” means one rank occurs twice and the other three cards have three different ranks. “Exactly two pairs” means two different ranks occur twice each, plus one card of a fifth rank.

Because the outcomes are finite and equally likely, probability is favorable hands divided by all hands. These categories are exact and disjoint: a hand with two pairs is not counted as exactly one pair.

WARM-UP · DENOMINATOR01

How many five-card hands?

Choose five cards from 52. Since the order in which they arrive does not change the hand, this is a combination, not an ordered sequence.

COUNT ALL HANDS(525)=52!5!47!=2,598,960
READ IT ALOUD

Fifty-two choose five counts the groups of five cards selected from 52, with each group counted once regardless of deal order.

Before continuing, explain why counting ordered deals would count the same hand more than once.

Worked reasoning

There are 52 choices for the first card, 51 for the next, and so on through 48. That counts each group in 5! = 120 orders, so divide by 120: (52 × 51 × 50 × 49 × 48) / (5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1) = 2,598,960. This is the common denominator for both probabilities.

TRY IT · EXACTLY ONE PAIR02

Count the one-pair hands

A hand of exactly one pair has a pattern like Q♠, Q♥, J♣, 5♦, 2♠: one rank appears twice, and three other distinct ranks appear once each.

Work out the choices in order: select the pair rank and its two suits; choose three other ranks; choose one suit for each. Why must those three ranks be different?

Hint

Build the hand in layers; do not start by multiplying yet.

  1. First mark the rank that appears twice. How many ranks could fill that role?
  2. For that rank, choose two of its four suited cards. Does swapping the order you name those two cards make a new pair?
  3. Now focus on the other three cards. To keep exactly one pair, what ranks must they avoid? They must avoid the pair rank and one another, so choose three distinct ranks from the remaining ranks.
  4. After choosing those ranks, how many suit choices does each one have?

Once you have a count for each layer, multiply the counts to get favorable hands. Only then divide by all five-card hands to get the probability.

Worked reasoning

Let the pair rank be, for example, Queen. The other three cards are the single cards (often called kickers). The word “exactly” places an important restriction on them: each must have a rank different from the Queen, and their three ranks must also be different from one another. Otherwise a single card would make another pair, or a third Queen would make three of a kind.

  1. Choose the rank of the pair. There are 13 ranks, so there are 13 choices. For example, choose Queen.
  2. Choose the two cards of that rank. The chosen rank has four suits. Choose any two: (42)=6. These are two distinct Queen cards; choosing Queen of hearts and Queen of spades is the same pair whichever one we name first.
  3. Choose the ranks of the other three cards. We must exclude the pair rank, leaving 12 ranks. Then choose 3 different ranks from those 12: (123)=220. This choice enforces both exclusions: none matches the pair rank, and no two single cards have the same rank. For example, after choosing Queens as the pair, ranks Jack, 5, and 2 work; Queen, Jack, Jack does not.
  4. Choose a suit for each of those ranks. Each selected rank has four cards, and we take one of them. For the three selected ranks this gives 4×4×4=43=64 suit assignments.

For example, once the ranks Queen, Jack, 5, and 2 are set, choosing one suit for each of Jack, 5, and 2 completes the hand. We multiply the four step-counts because every combination of these choices builds one hand. Conversely, each exactly-one-pair hand tells us exactly which four choices were made, so none are missed or counted twice.

FAVORABLE HANDS(131)·(42)·(123)·(41)3=13·6·220·64=1,098,240
READ IT ALOUD

Choose one of 13 ranks, two of its four suits, three of the 12 other ranks, and one of four suits for each singleton card; multiply these counts to get 1,098,240 hands.

The combination choices pick unordered rank/suit groups; the product rule multiplies the numbers of choices because each completed set of choices gives one hand. This does not assert that the drawing steps are independent random events.

Divide by all hands: 1,098,240 / 2,598,960 = 352 / 833 ≈ 0.422569. So the chance of exactly one pair is about 42.26%.

TRY IT · EXACTLY TWO PAIRS03

Count two pairs without double-counting

A hand like A♠, A♦, K♣, K♥, 7♠ has two paired ranks and one singleton card (the “kicker”). The two pair ranks form an unordered choice: swapping which rank you name first does not make a different hand.

Choose the two pair ranks, their suits, then the kicker's rank and suit. Why must the kicker rank be outside the chosen pair ranks?

Hint

Separate the hand into its rank pattern first: two ranks appear twice, and one different rank appears once.

  1. Choose the two ranks that will be paired. Is their order part of the hand, or are the two ranks just one unordered group?
  2. For each paired rank, choose two of its four suited cards.
  3. Choose the kicker's rank from ranks not already used. What would happen if it matched one of the pair ranks?
  4. Finally choose the kicker's suit.

Multiply the counts for these choices. Check that each set of choices makes one two-pair hand, and that every such hand can be described this way.

Worked reasoning

Now the hand has a different pattern: two ranks appear twice, and one card has a third rank. That last card is the kicker. Follow the choices carefully:

  1. Choose the two pair ranks. Choose 2 different ranks from 13: (132)=78. This is an unordered choice: Aces and Kings name the same pair of ranks as Kings and Aces.
  2. Choose the two suits for the first pair. From the four cards of its rank, choose 2: (42)=6.
  3. Choose the two suits for the second pair. There are also 6 choices for this different rank. Thus the two pairs' suit choices contribute 6×6=36. Since the pair ranks were chosen together, there is no “first pair / second pair” ordering to count.
  4. Choose the kicker's rank. The two pair ranks are already used, leaving 11 ranks. The kicker must come from one of these 11: if it shared either paired rank, that rank would appear three times and the hand would be a full house, not two pairs.
  5. Choose the kicker's suit. One card from its chosen rank can have any of four suits, so there are 4 choices.

For instance, after choosing Aces and Kings as the pair ranks, the kicker might be a 7. Any one of the four sevens completes the rank pattern. Multiplying 78 rank choices, 36 choices of suits for the pairs, 11 kicker ranks, and 4 kicker suits gives each two-pair hand exactly once.

FAVORABLE HANDS(132)·(42)2·(111)·(41)=78·6·6·11·4=123,552
READ IT ALOUD

Choose two ranks from 13, two suits from each chosen rank, one kicker rank from the 11 unused ranks, and one of its four suits; multiply to get 123,552 hands.

Divide by the same total: 123,552 / 2,598,960 = 198 / 4,165 ≈ 0.047539. The chance of exactly two pairs is about 4.75%.

There is no extra factor of two for ordering the pairs: the combination “13 choose 2” already selects them as one unordered pair of ranks.

Check your understanding

Both answers depend on defining the category precisely and counting every favorable hand once. The “exactly one pair” count excludes two-pair and full-house hands because the three remaining ranks must all be distinct and different from the paired rank. In the two-pair count, the kicker must have a third rank different from both paired ranks.

For another paper-first challenge, count (1) exactly three of a kind: three cards of one rank and two other cards of different ranks; (2) a full house: three cards of one rank and a pair of a different rank; and (3) a flush: all five cards have the same suit. For this exercise, include straight flushes in the flush count. For each hand, identify what you choose first, then what choices remain. Try all three before opening the answer.

Show short worked answers

Exactly three of a kind

Choose the triple's rank from 13, then three of its four suits. For the two remaining cards, choose two distinct ranks from the other 12, then one of four suits for each. The distinct ranks prevent another pair, keeping the hand exactly three of a kind.

FAVORABLE HANDS(131)·(43)·(122)·(41)2=54,912
READ IT ALOUD

Choose the triple's rank and suits, then two different other ranks and one suit for each; this gives 54,912 hands.

Divide by all hands: 54,912 / 2,598,960 = 88 / 4,165 ≈ 2.11%.

A full house

Choose the rank for the triple and three of its four suits. Then choose the pair rank from the 12 other ranks, and two of its four suits. Excluding the triple rank from the pair choice ensures the ranks are different.

FAVORABLE HANDS(131)·(43)·(121)·(42)=3,744
READ IT ALOUD

Choose the triple's rank and three suits, then a different rank for the pair and two suits; this gives 3,744 hands.

Divide by all hands: 3,744 / 2,598,960 = 6 / 4,165 ≈ 0.144%.

A flush (all five cards of one suit)

Choose the suit first: there are 4 choices. That suit contains 13 cards, so choose any 5 of them. This count includes straight flushes, as stated in the challenge.

FAVORABLE HANDS(41)·(135)=4·1,287=5,148
READ IT ALOUD

Choose one of four suits and five of its thirteen cards; there are 5,148 same-suit hands, including straight flushes.

Divide by all hands: 5,148 / 2,598,960 = 33 / 16,660 ≈ 0.198%.

All three counts use favorable hands divided by the same 2,598,960 equally likely hands.

Optional Python check

This uses Python's math.comb(n, r) for “n choose r” and Fraction to retain exact probability ratios. It checks the arithmetic; the counting argument above explains why these are the right counts.

Show code
from math import comb
from fractions import Fraction

all_hands = comb(52, 5)
one_pair = comb(13, 1) * comb(4, 2) * comb(12, 3) * 4**3
two_pairs = comb(13, 2) * comb(4, 2)**2 * comb(11, 1) * 4

print("All hands:", all_hands)
print("Exactly one pair:", one_pair, Fraction(one_pair, all_hands))
print("Exactly two pairs:", two_pairs, Fraction(two_pairs, all_hands))

Expected output: all hands 2,598,960; exactly one pair 1,098,240 and 352/833; exactly two pairs 123,552 and 198/4165. The program does not enumerate hands or prove that the case breakdown is complete.
