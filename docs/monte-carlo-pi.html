WORKED APPLICATION · GEOMETRY AND PROBABILITY

Can random points estimate π?

A quarter circle inside a square turns an area ratio into a probability. We can derive that ratio first, then approximate it by counting random points.

SUBJECTS USEDProbabilityMonte CarloPowers

First turn geometry into a chance.

Choose a point (x, y) uniformly from the square whose horizontal and vertical coordinates both run from 0 to 1. “Uniformly” means equal-area parts of the square are equally likely. The quarter of the unit circle in that square contains points at distance at most 1 from the origin. By the Pythagorean distance rule, the square of that distance is x2+y2; distance at most 1 is therefore equivalent to the test below.

INSIDE THE QUARTER CIRCLE0≤x,y≤1,x2+y2≤1
READ IT ALOUD

For a point in the unit square, add the squares of its coordinates. If the result is at most one, it lies in the quarter circle.

Why π divided by four?

The square has width 1 and height 1, so its area is 1 × 1 = 1. For a circle of positive radius r, the familiar area formula is πr2. Here r = 1 and we use a quarter of the circle, so its area is π×124=π4. This is a geometry fact we use as a prerequisite, not a derivation of the circle-area formula.

For a uniformly chosen point, the chance of landing in a region is its area divided by the square's area. The boundary curve has zero area, so including or excluding points exactly on it does not change the ideal probability.

EXACT MODEL PROBABILITYP(inside)=(π/4)/1=π4
READ IT ALOUD

The chance a uniform point lands inside is the quarter-circle area divided by the square area, or pi divided by four.

Count hits to estimate, not prove, π

Take N independently chosen uniform points. Count H “hits” inside the quarter circle. The observed hit fraction HN estimates the model probability π4; multiply the fraction by 4 to estimate π. N must be positive, and the sample must follow the uniform independent model for this interpretation.

MONTE CARLO ESTIMATEπ^=4HN
READ IT ALOUD

Estimated pi equals four times the number of points inside, divided by the total number sampled.

For a hand-picked illustration, the eight points (0.1, 0.2), (0.3, 0.4), (0.6, 0.7), (0.9, 0.9), (0.8, 0.2), (0.2, 0.9), (0.7, 0.7), and (1, 0.5) have six hits: for example, (0.6, 0.7) is inside because 0.36 + 0.49 = 0.85 ≤ 1, whereas (0.9, 0.9) is outside because 0.81 + 0.81 = 1.62 > 1. The count gives 4×68=3. These chosen points demonstrate the test, not a random experiment or evidence that π is 3.

In a real simulation, more independent samples usually reduce typical sampling fluctuation, but no finite run is guaranteed to get closer, and a repeatable pseudorandom seed is not a mathematical proof. This method illustrates probability and area; it is not a competitive way to calculate many digits of π.

What this calculation does—and does not—show

The geometry gives the exact relationship P(inside)=π4 under the uniform model. The observed ratio HN is an estimate from finitely many points; even agreement with math.pi on one run does not prove the geometry or certify a number of correct digits. Try a different seed or N and compare the estimates.
