CAPSTONE · VECTORS · CURVES

Follow a control point through a curve.

A Bézier curve turns a small set of control points into a smooth path. We will use three points to read the curve in two ways: first as a weighted blend, then as an algebraic expression. The small example shows what the larger formula is doing.

SUBJECTS USEDVectorsPolynomialsBernstein basis

A quadratic curve needs three control points.

A control point is a point that helps shape a curve. A quadratic curve has polynomial degree 2—the highest exponent in its polynomial—and uses 3 control points. Here, “degree” counts powers, not angles. In computer graphics and vector drawing, a designer can move these points to adjust the path.

OUR CONTROL POINTSP0=(0,0),P1=(2,2),P2=(4,0)
READ IT ALOUD

The first point P zero is at zero, zero. P one is at two, two. The last point P two is at four, zero.

The curve starts at P0 and ends at P2. The middle point P1 pulls the path upward, but the curve does not generally pass through that middle control point.

The parameter t tells us where we are as we move along the curve. We use values from 0 to 1: at 0 we are at the start, and at 1 we are at the end.

A polynomial is a finite sum or difference of terms. A term is one piece: a coefficient times a variable raised to a nonnegative whole-number power (0, 1, 2, and so on). For the curve above, the y-coordinate 4t−4t2 has two terms: 4t and −4t2. A one-term polynomial is a monomial, a two-term polynomial is a binomial, and a three-term polynomial is a trinomial. These are all polynomials; the names distinguish their term counts. Expressions with more terms are still called polynomials. This example uses polynomial expressions, not trigonometric functions such as sine or cosine; t is a parameter from 0 to 1, not an angle.

How the formulas fit together

Now that we have named the control points and the parameter that moves along the curve, here is how the formulas relate. There are two equivalent ways to calculate a point: Bernstein form and power form. The coefficient formula converts between them; the quadratic equation and sample calculations are particular cases; pixel mapping is separate. This is a map of relationships, not a sequence to perform. For rendering, the example uses Bernstein form to calculate points, then maps them to screen coordinates.

  • Two equivalent ways to calculate a curve point:
    • Bernstein form combines the control points using weights, so their contributions are easy to see. The quadratic formula below is this same form with the degree set to 2. The sampling code uses Bernstein form directly.
    • Power form expands the same curve into ordinary powers of its parameter, with a vector coefficient (the vector multiplied by each power). Its familiar polynomial shape is useful for algebraic work, such as finding how steep the curve is at a point. The halfway point can be calculated with either form.
  • Converting Bernstein to power form:
    • The coefficient formula calculates the vector values that multiply each power, using the control points. Its derivation is the algebraic proof that the forms agree, and the formula itself is a conversion recipe—not a third curve.
    • The factorial ratio and finite product are equivalent ways to write one factor in that recipe; the product writes out the consecutive factors being multiplied.
    • The worked coefficient values show the conversion applied to this quadratic example.
  • Pixel mapping is a separate graphics step, after calculating a curve point, that converts its coordinates to a screen column and row.
When is each useful?
  • Use Bernstein form to understand how control points contribute and to generate points for this rendering example.
  • Use power form when polynomial algebra is useful, for example when studying the curve's slope or area.
  • Use the coefficient formula only when you need to convert control points into power coefficients. Its derivation explains why the forms agree; you do not need it just to draw the curve.

Bernstein form: blend the control points.

The Bézier formula combines the control points. The index i visits them one at a time; n is the degree. The combination number (ni) is a binomial coefficient from the combinations lesson.

BERNSTEIN FORMB(t)=∑i=0n(ni)(1−t)n−itiPi
READ IT ALOUD

The point B of t is the sum, as i goes from zero through n, of n choose i times one minus t to the n minus i, times t to the i, times control point P i.

The factors multiplying each control point are called Bernstein weights. When t is strictly between 0 and 1, each weight is nonnegative. The binomial theorem shows that all the weights add to 1:

THE WEIGHTS ADD TO ONE∑i=0n(ni)(1−t)n−iti=((1−t)+t)n=1
READ IT ALOUD

The sum of the Bernstein weights, as i goes from zero to n, equals one: one minus t plus t, all to the n, equals one.

So for a parameter strictly between 0 and 1, the curve point is a blend of the control points whose weights total one. For our degree-2 curve, n is 2 and there are 3 control points. Setting n to 2 in the general Bernstein form gives this quadratic case; its combination numbers are 1, 2, and 1:

QUADRATIC CASEB(t)=(1−t)2P0+2(1−t)tP1+t2P2
READ IT ALOUD

B of t equals one minus t squared times P zero, plus two times one minus t times t times P one, plus t squared times P two.

At t=12, the three weights are one quarter, one half, and one quarter. They add to 1, so the curve point is a weighted blend of the three control points:

HALFWAY POINTB(12)=14P0+12P1+14P2=(2,1)
READ IT ALOUD

Halfway along, the curve point is one quarter of P zero, plus one half of P one, plus one quarter of P two. The result is the point two, one.

So at the halfway parameter the curve is at (2,1), while the middle control point is (2,2). This illustrates the difference between a control point and a point on the curve.

Power form: expand the same curve into powers of t.

Now expand that same quadratic Bernstein formula to get the equivalent power form for our example. Substitute the control points and work with the x- and y-coordinates separately, since adding and scaling vectors means doing the same arithmetic to each coordinate:

SUBSTITUTE THE POINTSB(t)=(1−t)2(0,0)+2(1−t)t(2,2)+t2(4,0)
READ IT ALOUD

B of t is one minus t squared times the point zero, zero; plus two times one minus t times t times the point two, two; plus t squared times the point four, zero.

Expand the x-coordinate: the first point contributes 0, the middle point contributes 4(1−t)t, and the last contributes 4t2. For the y-coordinate, the middle point contributes 4(1−t)t and the other two contribute 0. Simplifying gives:

POWER FORMB(t)=(4t,4t−4t2)
READ IT ALOUD

B of t has x-coordinate four t and y-coordinate four t minus four t squared.

The same point is now described by ordinary powers of t. At t=12, this gives (2,1), matching the weighted-blend calculation above.

The coefficient formula converts between the forms.

The example's power form above came from directly expanding its quadratic Bernstein formula. For any degree, the coefficient formula below gives this same conversion from control points to the power form; its derivation shows why the conversion works. In the power form, the outer index j chooses the power of t, and Cj is the vector coefficient attached to that power. A coefficient is a multiplier; here, the coefficient itself is a vector. The scalar tj scales each coordinate of that vector, and the resulting vectors are added coordinate by coordinate. This is scalar-times-vector scaling, not vector-times-vector multiplication.

POWER-BASIS FORMB(t)=∑j=0ntjCj
READ IT ALOUD

B of t is the sum, as j goes from zero to n, of t to the j times coefficient vector C j.

To find Cj, expand the factor (1−t)n−i using the binomial theorem. In that expansion, r counts how many factors contribute a −t:

BINOMIAL THEOREM STEP(1−t)n−i=∑r=0n−i(n−ir)(−1)rtr
READ IT ALOUD

One minus t to the n minus i equals the sum, as r goes from zero to n minus i, of n minus i choose r times negative one to the r times t to the r.

The original term also contains ti. Multiplying powers adds their exponents, so a term contributes to the coefficient of tj when i+r=j, meaning r is j minus i. Thus, for a fixed j, add the contributions from control points i=0,1,…,j:

COEFFICIENT AS A SUMCj=∑i=0j(ni)(n−ij−i)(−1)j−iPi
READ IT ALOUD

C j is the sum, as i goes from zero through j, of n choose i times n minus i choose j minus i, times negative one to the j minus i, times P i.

Use the factorial definition of each combination coefficient. The shared (n−i)! cancels:

CANCEL THE SHARED FACTORIAL(ni)(n−ij−i)=n!i!(n−i)!(n−i)!(j−i)!(n−j)!=n!i!(j−i)!(n−j)!
READ IT ALOUD

The two combination coefficients equal n factorial divided by i factorial times n minus i factorial, multiplied by n minus i factorial divided by j minus i factorial times n minus j factorial. The shared n minus i factorial cancels.

This leaves the common factor n factorial over n minus j factorial, along with i factorial and j minus i factorial in the summand. Also, j minus i and i plus j differ by an even number, so their powers of negative one agree:

SIMPLIFIED COEFFICIENTCj=n!(n−j)!∑i=0j(−1)i+jPii!(j−i)!
READ IT ALOUD

C j equals n factorial divided by n minus j factorial, times the sum from i zero through j of negative one to i plus j times P i, divided by i factorial times j minus i factorial.

The factor n!(n−j)! is a shortened product: cancel the factorials from (n−j)! in n factorial, leaving j descending factors.

FACTORIAL RATIO AS A PRODUCTn!(n−j)!=∏m=0j−1(n−m)
READ IT ALOUD

N factorial over n minus j factorial equals the product, as m goes from zero to j minus one, of n minus m.

Replace the factorial ratio in the coefficient formula with its product form to get the full expression:

COEFFICIENT WITH A FINITE PRODUCTCj=(∏m=0j−1(n−m))∑i=0j(−1)i+jPii!(j−i)!
READ IT ALOUD

C j equals the product, as m goes from zero to j minus one, of n minus m, times the sum, as i goes from zero through j, of negative one to i plus j times P i divided by i factorial times j minus i factorial.

At j = 0, there are no factors to multiply. This is an empty product, defined to equal 1, just as an empty sum is defined to equal 0. The coefficient then simplifies to C0=P0, so the curve's constant term is its starting control point.

Use the conversion formula for this quadratic curve.

Here n is 2, so the power index j takes the values 0, 1, and 2. The inner index i selects which of the first j control points contributes to that coefficient.

  • j=0: C0=P0=(0,0).
  • j=1: C1=2(P1−P0)=(4,4).
  • j=2: C2=P0−2P1+P2=(0,−4).

Put these coefficient vectors back into the polynomial:

POLYNOMIAL FROM ITS COEFFICIENTSB(t)=C0+tC1+t2C2=(4t,4t−4t2)
READ IT ALOUD

B of t equals C zero plus t times C one plus t squared times C two. For these points, that is four t in the x-coordinate and four t minus four t squared in the y-coordinate.

For example, C1=(4,4), so tC1=(4t,4t): the scalar t multiplies each component. Similarly, C2=(0,−4) gives t2C2=(0,−4t2). These scaled vectors are added component by component to give the displayed power form; no vector is multiplied by another vector. The coefficient formula is longer because it works for any degree and any list of control points.

Walk the curve, then map its points to pixels.

The polynomial gives a point for every parameter value from 0 to 1. A computer display has finitely many pixels, so a simple renderer samples a sequence of t values, calculates each point, and connects neighboring samples with short line segments.

Use four equal parameter steps for this small demonstration. That gives five samples, including the start and end. For example, at t = one quarter, substitute into both coordinates:

ONE CURVE SAMPLEB(14)=(4·14,4·14−4·(14)2)=(1,34)
READ IT ALOUD

At t equals one quarter, the curve point has x-coordinate one and y-coordinate three quarters.

Next map mathematical coordinates to a small pixel canvas. Put the mathematical origin at pixel (20, 100), and use 40 pixels for each coordinate unit. Screen y-values increase downward, so subtract the mathematical y-coordinate from the baseline:

COORDINATES TO PIXELS(xpixel,ypixel)=(20+40x,100−40y)
READ IT ALOUD

The horizontal pixel coordinate is twenty plus forty times x. The vertical pixel coordinate is one hundred minus forty times y.

These coordinates fit in a 200-by-120-pixel canvas. The curve spans pixel columns 20 to 180 and rows 60 to 100; the control point P1 maps to row 20.

Connecting these five pixel positions gives a rough drawing of the path. Real renderers use more samples or a dedicated curve algorithm, then choose nearby pixels (often with anti-aliasing). Equal steps in t do not always mean equal distances along the curve.

A useful numerical caution

The algebraic power form is correct, but for very high degrees its terms can be large and cancel when added. That can reduce numerical accuracy in ordinary floating-point computing. The Bernstein form or de Casteljau's algorithm is often preferred for practical curve evaluation; de Casteljau is a later topic, not a different definition of the curve.

FORM USED BY THIS CODEB(t)=∑i=0n(ni)(1−t)n−itiPi

The code sums each control point P i multiplied by its Bernstein weight to get B of t.

In the code, curve_degree is the mathematical n: the curve's polynomial degree (the highest power in its power form), not an angle measure. It is calculated as the number of control points minus one. Thus these three control points give a degree of 2. comb(curve_degree, i) is the coefficient “n choose i”; control_point is the current point Pi; and weight is (ni)(1−t)n−iti. Read the sampling loop's bounds and weights. Python's range stops before its endpoint, so range(steps + 1) visits sample indices 0 through 4 and includes both endpoints of the parameter interval. Four intervals therefore produce five points. control_points[i] is Pi; curve_degree is n, computed as len(control_points) - 1; and weight is the Bernstein factor for that point. Python's comb(n, i) supplies the same binomial coefficient as “n choose i.” The chosen scale makes these sample positions exact whole-pixel coordinates, so int() leaves them unchanged while ensuring the pixel coordinates are integers. For other values, Python's int() truncates toward zero rather than rounding to the nearest pixel; a real renderer needs an appropriate rounding or pixel-coverage rule.
