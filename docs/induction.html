PROOFS · MATHEMATICAL INDUCTION

Prove the first step and the next step.

The previous lesson showed why a loop keeps its promise on every pass. Induction uses a similar pattern to prove a mathematical claim for every nonnegative integer—not just the few values we can test.

A claim about a familiar sum

Adding the integers from 1 through n gives the same answer as multiplying n by the next integer, then dividing by 2. Here n is any nonnegative integer. We will prove this formula, not use it without explanation.

CLAIM∑i=1ni=n(n+1)2
READ IT ALOUD

Add each integer from one through n. The result equals n times the quantity n plus one, all divided by two.

For example, with n = 4, adding one, two, three, and four gives ten. Four times five, divided by two, also gives ten. That check helps us see the claim, but one example does not prove it for every n.

1. Check the starting case

We start at n = 0, matching the nonnegative inputs of the summation loop. The sum has no terms and equals zero; the formula also gives zero:

BASE CASE∑i=10i=0=0(0+1)2
READ IT ALOUD

The empty sum is zero, and the formula gives zero too. The claim is true at the starting value.

2. Show that one true case leads to the next

Let k stand for an arbitrary nonnegative integer. Assume the formula is true when n equals k. This temporary assumption is the induction hypothesis; we are not assuming the entire claim is true. We must use it to prove the case k + 1.

ASSUME FOR k∑i=1ki=k(k+1)2
READ IT ALOUD

Assume adding one through k gives k times the quantity k plus one, all divided by two.

To get the next sum, add the next term k + 1 to the sum through k. Now replace that earlier sum using the hypothesis:

ADD ONE TERM∑i=1k+1i=k(k+1)2+(k+1)
READ IT ALOUD

To sum through k plus one, use the assumed result for the sum through k, then add one new term: k plus one.

Put both terms over denominator 2, factor out their common k + 1, then simplify:

SIMPLIFYk(k+1)+2(k+1)2=(k+1)(k+2)2
READ IT ALOUD

Factor out k plus one; the remaining k plus two gives the same formula with k plus one in place of n.

In the original formula, putting n = k + 1 gives the two factors k + 1 and k + 2. That is exactly what we obtained, so the assumption for k really does imply the next case.

3. Connect the steps

The claim holds at 0. If it holds at any nonnegative integer k, the step above shows it holds at k + 1. Thus it holds at 1, then 2, then 3, and so on for every nonnegative integer. This is mathematical induction. The base case alone is not enough; neither is the next-step argument without a base case.

This is related to a loop invariant: the loop-correctness proof also establishes a starting truth, keeps it true after a step, and checks what it means at the end. Here we prove a formula about all nonnegative integers rather than a claim about program state.
