WORKED APPLICATION · WORD COUNTS AND VECTORS

How similar are these word lists?

Start with two tiny sentences, turn them into vectors by counting words in a shared order, then use their dot product and lengths to calculate one comparison score.

SUBJECTS USEDVectors and dot productsPowers and rootsCosine similarity

Build the vectors before doing any algebra.

Take sentence A, “cats chase mice,” and sentence B, “cats chase birds.” For this toy example, lowercase the words and separate them at spaces; neither sentence has punctuation. For other text, punctuation would need an explicit rule. Use each distinct word from either sentence once, in this fixed order: cats, chase, mice, birds. This shared list is the vocabulary. A vector entry counts occurrences of its word; a zero means that word is absent. Both vectors must use the same vocabulary and order.

Construct each entry by tallying its word in the sentence

A: cats chase mice1110
B: cats chase birds1101

For example, A contains “mice” once but not “birds,” so its third entry is 1 and its fourth is 0. B instead has 0 then 1. Name their vectors a and b in this example:

COUNT VECTORSa=(1,1,1,0),b=(1,1,0,1)
READ IT ALOUD

A counts cats, chase, and mice once; B counts cats, chase, and birds once. Both lists follow the same four-word order.

How many matching word occurrences?

To compute the dot product, multiply counts in matching positions and add. The first two words contribute 1 each; “mice” and “birds” contribute 0 because each is missing from one sentence.

DOT PRODUCTa·b=1×1+1×1+1×0+0×1=2
READ IT ALOUD

Multiply corresponding word counts and add. The two shared words contribute two in total.

The dot product is a count-weighted overlap, not yet a normalized similarity: a longer text could get a larger dot product simply by containing more words.

Account for the sizes of the vectors

A vector's length, also called its norm, is the square root of the sum of its squared components. Square each count, add the squares, then take the square root. The bars around a vector below mean its length, not a probability or the number of words in the sentence. Each vector here has three 1s and one 0:

VECTOR LENGTHS
‖a‖=12+12+12+02=3
‖b‖=12+12+02+12=3
READ IT ALOUD

A's length is the square root of three, and B's length is the square root of three as well.

Neither vector is all zeros, so neither length is zero. An empty or all-zero vector cannot be used in the division below: we would be dividing by zero.

Divide the overlap by the two lengths

Cosine similarity answers: how aligned are two nonzero vectors, after accounting for their lengths? For this word-count example, divide the dot product 2 by the product of the two lengths. The name comes from a geometric interpretation involving angles; we do not need to calculate an angle here.

COSINE SIMILARITY
cosSim(a,b)=a·b‖a‖‖b‖
cosSim(a,b)=233=23
READ IT ALOUD

Cosine similarity is the dot product divided by both vector lengths. Here that is two divided by three, or about 0.667.

The last step uses square root of 3 times square root of 3 = 3. The result is a similarity score, not a probability. For nonnegative count vectors the cosine score lies from 0 to 1: zero for no shared counted words, one for vectors with the same proportions. General bounds on dot products explain that upper limit; we use it here without proving the general inequality. For arbitrary vectors with negative entries, cosine similarity can also be negative.

What is this score missing?

“Mice chase cats” has the same word-count vector as “cats chase mice” and would score 1 against sentence A, even though word order—and potentially the meaning—has changed. Counts also miss synonyms and context. This toy calculation is not a trained language model or a guarantee of semantic similarity; different text-processing choices can produce different vectors and scores.
