PRACTICE · PROBABILITY AND COUNTING

Keep, reroll, and count

In Yatzy, you see your dice, choose which to keep, and reroll the others. Let’s use a small outcome grid to see how probability counting works, then carry the same reasoning into a reroll decision.

SUBJECTS USEDProbabilityCombinationsFactorialsIndexed sums
THE MODEL

Rules for these exercises

  • Five fair six-sided dice. Each die has faces 1 through 6; each face is equally likely.
  • A turn has one initial roll and up to two rerolls. You may choose which dice to reroll.
  • Unused rerolls cannot be saved. Each reroll uses a new independent roll.
  • For a small straight, the five faces must be exactly 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. We focus on chances, not score totals.

These exercises use the probability model that each die roll is fair and independent. Independence means earlier results do not change the chance of a six on a later roll.

WARM-UP01

A second try at a six

You roll one die and hope for a six. It is not a six. You roll again. What is the chance that the second roll is a six?

Try it on paper: did the first result make the next roll more or less likely to be a six?

Hint

Imagine starting with a fresh fair die. The previous result does not change its six faces or their chances.

Worked reasoning

The second roll has a one-in-six chance of showing six. The first roll being a failure does not change the second roll's probability:

P(six on second roll)=16

If instead the question were “a six on both rolls,” we would multiply: one sixth times one sixth equals one thirty-sixth. If it were “at least one six in two rolls,” we would count a six on the first roll, or a failure followed by a six: one sixth plus five sixths times one sixth, which is eleven thirty-sixths. Those are different events.

Takeaway: independent chances do not add. Multiplication follows a path of outcomes; addition combines disjoint ways the event can happen.

How many boxes are checked? How many boxes are there altogether? Be careful: the denominator is all boxes, not only the unchecked boxes.

Hint

The grid has six rows and six columns. The sixes in row 6 and column 6 overlap in one box, so do not count that box twice.

Worked reasoning

There are 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely outcomes. Five checked boxes are in column 6 above the last row, and six more are in row 6, for 11 checked boxes. The shared (6, 6) box is counted only once.

P(at least one six)=1136

“Checked boxes divided by all boxes” is probability. Checked boxes divided by unchecked boxes would instead be the odds in favor: 11 to 25.

We can also count the unchecked boxes. Both dice must be 1 through 5, giving 5 × 5 = 25 unchecked outcomes. So 36 − 25 = 11 outcomes are favorable. This is the complement method: count everything, subtract the cases where the event does not happen.

STRETCH · GENERALIZE THE RULE03

From a grid to n dice with m faces

Now replace the six-sided dice with n fair dice, each with m faces. Here n is the number of dice, and m is the number of faces on each die. We want at least one die to show a specified target face (six was the target face in the earlier example).

Before opening the reasoning, try this: how many outcomes have no specified face? Why is that easier to count?

Worked reasoning

Each die has m possible faces, so n independent dice have m choices repeated n times: mn equally likely ordered outcomes. If one specified face is forbidden, each die has only m − 1 choices, giving (m − 1)n outcomes with no success.

Subtract the outcomes with no success from all outcomes. The probability is:

P(at least one specified face)=1−(m−1m)n

Read it aloud: “The probability of at least one specified face is one minus the quantity m minus one over m, raised to n.” The denominator counts all possible outcomes; the subtracted fraction is the chance that every die misses the specified face.

For two six-sided dice, this becomes 1 − (5/6)2 = 11/36, matching the checked boxes. The picture helps us see the small case; the counting argument explains why the rule extends to any positive number of fair, independent dice with the same number of faces.

What generalizes: the total number of equally likely outcomes is mn. The number of favorable outcomes depends on the event; here we used the complement, “no die shows the specified face.”

More examples: same faces, different orders. Three six-sided dice are rolled. How many ordered outcomes give the face pattern 2, 3, 3? List them, then compare that count with all 63 outcomes.

Show the count for 2, 3, 3

The three rolls occupy three distinct positions, so these are different outcomes: (2, 3, 3), (3, 2, 3), and (3, 3, 2). There are three, not one, ordered outcomes for that face pattern.

This face pattern has one 2 and two 3s. Label the dice positions first, second, and third. Choose which one position shows 2; the other two positions must show 3. The choices are first, second, or third, giving three rolls. In “three choose one,” the 3 is the number of available positions, and the 1 is the number of positions we choose for the 2. This notation names a count of choices.

(31)=3!1!(3−1)!=3!1!2!=3

Read it aloud: “Three choose one equals three factorial divided by one factorial times the factorial of the quantity three minus one.” The parentheses matter: it is (3 − 1)!, or 2!, not 3! − 1!. This is the same count as the repeated-face formula: the 2! accounts for the two identical 3s, whose swap does not make a new outcome. The combinations lesson explains the “choose” notation; factorial notation is reviewed in Products and factorials.

If all three values are different, as in 1, 2, 3, each of the six orders is possible. If all are the same, as in 4, 4, 4, there is only one order. Repeated values reduce the number of distinct orders.

See how this count generalizes to n rolls and m faces

These are two different formulas for two different questions. The earlier formula gives the probability of getting at least one target face. The factorial formula below counts how many ordered rolls match one exact face pattern, 2, 3, 3, in any order. It is a count, not yet a probability; divide that count by all 63 rolls to get the probability.

For the pattern 2, 3, 3, there are n = 3 rolls and m = 6 possible face values. Let f name a face value (1 through 6 here; in general, 1 through m). Let cf be the number of dice showing face f in this target pattern:

Same counts, written in face order:

c1 counts ones, c2 counts twos, c3 counts threes, and so on. The subscript is a lookup label, not multiplication.

Build the list by making one slot per face, starting every count at zero, then adding one to the slot whenever a die shows that face. Thus cf is the number of dice showing face f.

The counts add up to the number of rolls: 0 + 1 + 2 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 3. In general, the sum of the counts for all m face values is the number n of dice:

∑f=1mcf=n

Read it aloud: “Add the counts for faces one through m; the total is n dice.” This sum checks that every die is counted once. It does not count the different orders; the factorial formula does that.

Why divide by factorials? If all n values were different, there would be n! orders. But swapping identical copies of the same face does not create a new roll sequence. If face f occurs cf times, divide out the cf! swaps of those copies. Do this for each face.

So, for any fixed face pattern, the number of distinct orders is:

n!c1!·c2!·…·cm!

Read it aloud: “n factorial divided by the product of c one factorial, c two factorial, and so on through c m factorial.” Here that is 3!/(0!·1!·2!·0!·0!·0!) = 3!/(1!·2!) = 3. Counts of zero contribute 0! = 1, so they can be left out. This is the same as the “three choose one” count: choose which die shows 2, and the other two must show 3.

There are 3 favorable ordered rolls out of 63 = 216 total rolls. So the probability of this exact face pattern in any order is 3/216 = 1/72. The factorial formula counts favorable rolls; dividing by all equally likely rolls gives probability.

YATZY · KEEP OR REROLL?04

One die away from a small straight

Your initial roll is 1, 2, 3, 4, 6. You want the small straight 1–2–3–4–5. You have two rerolls left.

12346

KEEP 1, 2, 3, 4 REROLL 6

What is the chance of completing the straight if you reroll only the 6, and if needed reroll that same die once more?

Hint

Think in two disjoint cases: get a 5 on the first reroll; or miss on the first reroll, then get a 5 on the second. A miss is any of five faces.

Worked reasoning

Keep the 1, 2, 3, and 4: they already provide four of the five faces needed. Reroll the 6 and look for a 5. Each attempt has chance 1/6 of success and 5/6 of missing.

There are two ways to succeed: get a 5 immediately, or miss once and then get a 5. The paths do not overlap:

P(straight)=16+56·16=1136

The same answer comes from the complement: fail both times, which has chance (5/6) × (5/6) = 25/36. Therefore success at least once is 1 − 25/36 = 11/36.

“The second reroll” still has a 1/6 chance of showing 5. The factor 5/6 appears because that path requires the first reroll to miss before the second attempt happens. If the first reroll succeeds, the turn is already complete; unused rerolls are not saved or transferred.

What the pictures do—and do not—need to do

The 6×6 grid makes two dice easy to inspect. Three dice could be shown as six such grids, one for each value of the third die. For more dice, we do not need to hold a higher-dimensional picture in mind: each new independent die adds m choices to every existing outcome, so the count multiplies by m. A visual model can help us discover a pattern; the counting argument is what justifies extending it.

These answers rely on fair dice and independent rolls. The reroll example calculates the chance for the stated choice—keep four dice and reroll the other one. It does not prove that this is the best choice for every hand or every scoring goal.
