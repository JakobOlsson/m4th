SYNTHESIS · SUMS AND FACTORIALS

When each term is a factorial

We have already learned how to add indexed terms and how factorials multiply positive integers. Now we put those ideas together: the index chooses a value, and the whole factorial of that value is the term we add.

SUBJECTS USEDSummationFactorials

Read the whole summand at each index.

In summation notation, the bounds tell us which values the index takes. The expression beside the sigma sign tells us what to calculate at each value. Here that expression is k factorial, introduced in the factorial lesson.

EXAMPLE∑k=14k!
READ IT ALOUD

Add the factorials of the integers from one through four.

First substitute each index value

The index k takes the values 1, 2, 3, and 4. At each step, replace k in the whole term k!; do not add just the index values.

EXPAND∑k=14k!=1!+2!+3!+4!
READ IT ALOUD

One factorial, plus two factorial, plus three factorial, plus four factorial.

Now expand each factorial as a product. The factorial marks the end of a multiplication sequence down to one:

Index kTerm k!Value added
111
22 × 12
33 × 2 × 16
44 × 3 × 2 × 124

Add the four values: 1 + 2 + 6 + 24 = 33. So the sum of factorials is 33. Notice the two layers of work: each factorial multiplies, and the outer sum adds those factorial values.

RESULT∑k=14k!=1+2+6+24=33
READ IT ALOUD

The factorial terms evaluate to one, two, six, and twenty-four; their sum is thirty-three.

How the two layers become loops

The outer loop chooses k, the value whose factorial is the next term. The inner loop multiplies from 1 through k to calculate that factorial; factor is its changing counter. Python's range stops before its upper bound, so n + 1 and k + 1 make the loops include 4 and k, respectively. Then the outer loop adds the completed term to total. The inner multiplication starts at 1 because 1 leaves a product unchanged; the outer sum starts at 0 because adding 0 leaves a total unchanged.

A reusable way to read a more complex sum

  1. Read the bounds to find the index values.
  2. Substitute each index into the entire summand.
  3. Evaluate each term, then combine the terms using the sum's operation.

The same approach works when the term is a power, a probability, or another expression: first expand a few terms explicitly so it is clear what the index changes and what gets added.

Where this leads

This is a first example of a sum whose terms are themselves calculated expressions. The same pattern appears in probability formulas and in sums of products, where each term may take more unpacking.
