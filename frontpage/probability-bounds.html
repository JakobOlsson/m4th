PROBABILITY BOUNDS · THE UNION BOUND

Adding event chances gives an upper bound.

When we ask whether at least one of several events happens, adding their probabilities gives a safe upper bound. If events overlap, that sum can count some probability more than once.

Read the union bound.

The union of events means that at least one of them happens. For events A1 through An, the union bound says:

NOTATION

P(A1 ∪ A2 ∪ ⋯ ∪ An) ≤ ∑i=1n P(Ai)

The symbol ∪ means “or”: at least one listed event occurs.

READ IT ALOUD

“The probability that at least one of the events A one through A n happens is at most the sum, as i goes from one through n, of the probability of event A sub i.”

No independence assumption is needed.

The inequality holds whether or not the events are independent, and whether or not they overlap. Events are independent when learning that one occurred does not change the chance of another. Independence may help us calculate probabilities, but it is not required for this bound.

EXPANDED TWO-EVENT CASE

P(A1 ∪ A2) ≤ P(A1) + P(A2)

READ IT ALOUD

“The probability of A one or A two is at most the probability of A one plus the probability of A two.”

Two overlapping events on one die.

Roll a fair six-sided die and let X be the result. Let A1 mean “the result is even,” and A2 mean “the result is greater than 3.” Mark a check when a die result belongs to an event:

Read the union column: results 2, 4, 5, and 6 belong to at least one event, so A1 ∪ A2 = {2, 4, 5, 6} and its probability is 4/6 = 2/3. Results 4 and 6 belong to both events, so they are counted twice in the sum on the right.

Each event has probability 3/6 = 1/2, so the bound says 2/3 ≤ 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. It is true, but not exact.

WHY THE TWO-EVENT BOUND HOLDS

Separate the new part of the second event.

The set difference A2 \ A1 means outcomes that are in A2 but not in A1. These outcomes do not overlap A1, and together the two pieces make the union:

DISJOINT DECOMPOSITION

A1 ∪ A2 = A1 ∪ (A2 \ A1)

READ IT ALOUD

“A one or A two is A one or the part of A two that is not in A one.”

Because the two pieces on the right are disjoint, their probabilities add. Also, A2 \ A1 is only part of A2, so its probability cannot exceed P(A2):

P(A1 ∪ A2) = P(A1) + P(A2 \ A1) ≤ P(A1) + P(A2).

Repeat the same reasoning as each event is added to extend the result to any finite number of events. If the events are disjoint, no probability is counted twice, and the bound is an equality.

Where this leads

The union bound is a basic tool for reasoning about many possible failure events: if any one can cause a system failure, add their probabilities to get a safe upper bound. Later, we can use it in algorithm analysis and reliability examples, and compare it with tighter bounds that use more information.
