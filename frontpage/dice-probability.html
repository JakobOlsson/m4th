SYNTHESIS · PROBABILITY AND COUNTING

At least two sixes in three rolls

We can answer this question by combining three familiar ideas: count which roll positions show sixes, multiply the probabilities for one such pattern, and add the disjoint cases for exactly two or exactly three sixes.

Define the event and count the possible outcomes.

Roll a fair six-sided die three times—equivalently, use three fair dice, one for each position. Treat the first, second, and third rolls as distinct positions, so a sequence such as (6, 2, 6) is different from (2, 6, 6). Let X count the number of rolls that show six; this is the random variable for the experiment, as introduced in the probability lesson. “At least two” means exactly two or exactly three of the rolls show six.

QUESTIONP(X≥2)
READ IT ALOUD

The probability that the number of sixes in the three rolls is at least two.

There are 216 equally likely roll sequences

Each roll has six possible faces. Because the die rolls are independent, every ordered triple has the same probability, one over six multiplied three times. The total number of possible sequences is:

ALL OUTCOMES63=216
READ IT ALOUD

Six choices on each of three rolls gives six cubed, or 216 ordered outcomes.

Here, fair means each of the six faces is equally likely, so a six has probability one sixth and a non-six has probability five sixths. The rolls are independent: the result of one roll does not change the probabilities on another roll.

Case 1: exactly two sixes

First choose which two of the three roll positions show six. This is a combination because we are choosing positions, and the combinations lesson explains why changing the order of the chosen positions does not make a new choice.

CHOOSE THE POSITIONS(32)=3
READ IT ALOUD

Choose two of the three roll positions in three ways.

For each choice of positions, the two chosen rolls must be sixes. The remaining roll must be a non-six, and it has five possible faces. So the combination count gives three position-patterns, with five outcomes for each pattern: 3×5=15 sequences with exactly two sixes.

COUNT THIS CASE(32)×5=3×5=15
WHAT IT COUNTS

Fifteen ordered sequences: three choices of which positions are sixes, and five possible non-six faces in the remaining position.

The combination alone is not the number of successful roll sequences: it counts only the position-patterns. Multiplying by five accounts for the remaining roll's possible faces.

Case 2: exactly three sixes

All three positions must show six. There is one way to choose all three positions, and only one sequence fits: (6, 6, 6).

COUNT THIS CASE(33)=1
WHAT IT COUNTS

One ordered sequence, namely six, six, six.

Combine the disjoint cases

The cases “exactly two sixes” and “exactly three sixes” cannot happen at the same time. Together they contain 15+1=16 favorable sequences. Since all 216 sequences were equally likely, the probability is the favorable count divided by the total count.

COUNTING RESULT15+1216=16216=227
READ IT ALOUD

Sixteen of the 216 equally likely sequences have at least two sixes. The probability is sixteen over 216, which simplifies to two twenty-sevenths.

As a decimal, this is about 0.0741≈7.41%. This is a probability for the stated model—a fair die and three independent rolls—not a guarantee about any particular set of three rolls.

Now express the same count as a sum of probabilities

Let k be the number of sixes. For a fixed value of k, choose which k positions show six. The chosen rolls each contribute a factor of one sixth; 3−k other rolls each contribute five sixths. Independence lets us multiply these probabilities for one pattern. The combination coefficient counts how many patterns there are. A power is repeated multiplication: for example, one sixth squared means one sixth times one sixth.

“At least two” includes the values k=2 and k=3, so we add the probability for each value:

GENERAL CASESP(X≥2)=∑k=23(3k)(16)k(56)3−k
READ IT ALOUD

The probability that X is at least two equals the sum, as k goes from two through three, of three choose k times one sixth to the k times five sixths to the three minus k.

Substitute the two possible values of k into the whole term. The first term is the exactly-two case; the second is the exactly-three case. When k is 3, there are no remaining non-six rolls, so there is no five-sixths factor to multiply (equivalently, that factor would be raised to the zero power and equal one).

EXPAND THE SUMP(X≥2)=(32)(16)2(56)+(33)(16)3=15216+1216=16216
READ IT ALOUD

The exactly-two term is fifteen over 216; the exactly-three term is one over 216. Add them to get sixteen over 216.

The count and probability-sum methods agree: both give 16 favorable sequences out of 216. The combination counts the positions; the probability factors account for the die faces in those positions.

A reusable way to combine counting and probability

  1. Translate “at least” into the exact counts that qualify.
  2. Use combinations to count which positions meet the condition.
  3. Account for the possible outcomes in the remaining positions.
  4. Add the probabilities of the separate cases.

Here, the number of sixes could be 2 or 3. The same pattern works for other numbers of rolls and other target outcomes, provided the probability model and independence assumptions are stated.

Keep the two counts distinct

“Three choose two” is 3 position-patterns, not 3 complete roll sequences. Each pattern has five possible faces for the remaining roll, giving 15 sequences with exactly two sixes. Counting what each number represents is what keeps the probability calculation honest.
